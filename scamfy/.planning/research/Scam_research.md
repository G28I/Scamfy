⚠️ Meaningful new student-relevant scam development: money-mule recruitment is becoming more directly tied to “help with loans” and account access.
A fresh September 10, 2026 police case reported by The Indian Express found 60+ women in Goa allegedly recruited under the pretext of helping them obtain loans, after which their bank accounts were passed to an international fraud network and used as mule accounts. The Indian Express
This matters for students because the same recruitment mechanism can plausibly be packaged as “loan assistance,” “part-time work,” “commission,” or “account verification” rather than openly asking someone to become a money mule. Earlier police reporting has specifically identified college students and unemployed youth among people targeted with account-rental offers. Hindustan Times
What the scam looks like
The dangerous pattern is:
Loan/job opportunity → request for bank-account details or account access → money routed through the account → account becomes associated with fraud.
Red flags include:
- “We need your account temporarily to process your loan.”
- “You will receive a commission for receiving/transferring money.”
- “Open an account for our company/client.”
- Someone asking for your debit card, cheque book, UPI credentials, OTP, or banking login.
- Being told that receiving money on behalf of someone else is harmless because you're “only an intermediary.”
This isn't theoretical. A February 2026 case involved a 19-year-old engineering student in Bengaluru whose account was allegedly used to launder ₹7 crore in two days after his account details were obtained by someone he knew. The Indian Express
Another student-specific development: social-media investment impersonation
A September 7, 2026 case involved a 23-year-old Pune law student who was allegedly lured through Snapchat by someone using a fake female identity and voice, promising cryptocurrency profits. The student ultimately transferred 1,468 USDT (~₹1.41 lakh) and received no returns. The profile photo was reportedly stolen from an influencer. The Indian Express
The important evolution isn't merely “crypto scam.” It's the combination of:
social-media identity → prolonged trust-building → investment opportunity → crypto transfer → disappearance.
That is particularly relevant to students who use Snapchat/Instagram/Telegram and may be approached socially before the financial pitch.
Official Indian guidance
National Cybercrime Reporting Portal
I4C currently provides the Suspect Search facility for checking reported mobile numbers, UPI IDs, bank accounts, email IDs and social-media identifiers. It explicitly warns that the database is based on citizen complaints, is incomplete, and is not proof that an identifier is guilty. Cyber Crime Portal
I4C Suspect Reporting Facility
Suspicious URLs, phone numbers, WhatsApp/Telegram handles, email IDs and social-media URLs can be reported there. Cyber Crime Portal
For financial cyber fraud, I4C says to report immediately through 1930 and/or the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal. Cyber Crime Portal
For fake job schemes specifically, I4C's CAPTCHA-job advisory warns about upfront registration/training/software fees, unrealistic earnings, denied withdrawals and additional “tax/processing” demands—a pattern highly relevant to students looking for part-time income. Cyber Crime Portal
Bottom line: the strongest new signal today is not a completely new scam category; it's the increasing convergence of loan assistance, easy-income recruitment and money-mule infrastructure, alongside increasingly convincing social-media investment impersonation targeting young people.

Student Scam Watch
⚠️ Meaningful new developments since the last check
1. Students themselves are now showing up in the money-mule recruitment chain
A 20 September 2026 Delhi case involved two college students allegedly helping route proceeds through mule accounts in a fake government-scholarship scam. One student allegedly arranged mule accounts for a 10% commission. Nation Press
The important pattern for students is:
scholarship/job/earning opportunity → “use/arrange an account” → commission → fraud proceeds move through the account.
That is materially different from merely being a victim of a scam: students can be recruited as infrastructure for the scam itself.
A separate 26 September 2026 Rajasthan case shows how organised this infrastructure can become: police allege that a facilitator arranged bank accounts, ATM cards, SIMs and passbook kits through Telegram groups, with mule accounts used alongside UPI, QR codes, wallets and crypto to layer stolen money. ThePrint
Rule for students: never let someone use your bank account, UPI ID, debit card, SIM, OTP or banking credentials to “receive money for them,” “process a payment,” or earn a commission.
2. RBI is moving toward a standardized response to suspected mule accounts
A September 11, 2026 RBI draft framework reportedly proposes standardized handling of accounts suspected of being linked to cyber fraud/money-mule activity, including temporary debit restrictions and a defined grievance process. The proposal follows the Supreme Court's August 2026 direction concerning prolonged freezing of innocent people's accounts. Indian Pay Calculator
This is important because being used as a mule isn't just a theoretical risk to the person whose account is recruited. An innocent account that receives fraud proceeds can also become entangled in investigations and banking restrictions.
Do not treat this as a final RBI rule yet; it is a draft proposal.
3. Fake investment scams are becoming more convincing, not just more aggressive
On 26 September 2026, a Hyderabad victim reportedly lost about ₹3.73 crore after being recruited through a WhatsApp advertisement into a fake investment platform. The website displayed fabricated profits and bonuses; the victim made 1,106 transactions over nine months, and withdrawal was blocked using additional conditions. Telangana Today
For students, the important pattern is:
social-media/WhatsApp advertisement → apparently legitimate investment interface → visible “profits” → increasing deposits → withdrawal problem → extra payment demanded.
This isn't a genuine investment-return signal. A number displayed on a website is not evidence that money exists.
I4C's investment-fraud advisory specifically warns about fake trading apps/websites, initial payouts used to establish trust, subsequent large deposits, and fraudulent WhatsApp/Telegram investment groups. Cyber Crime Portal
4. Fake recruitment remains a direct student/young-jobseeker threat
A 4 September 2026 Odisha case involved an organized fake-job racket targeting unemployed youths with fake appointment letters and IDs; police identified at least seven victims. ThePrint
More recently, a Delhi recruitment racket reportedly used candidate data obtained from a job-recruitment platform, then contacted victims with fake offers for companies including Tata Motors and demanded successive recruitment-related payments. Face2News
So the red flag isn't merely:
“Pay a registration fee.”

It's increasingly:
Your genuine job-search information is used to make the fake recruiter sound credible.

Students should independently verify the employer and recruiter through the company's official careers site before paying anything or submitting sensitive documents.
What to do if you've already been caught
Financial loss: call 1930 immediately and report it through the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal. I4C explicitly provides 1930 for immediate financial-fraud reporting. Cyber Crime Portal
National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal
For suspicious phone numbers, WhatsApp/Telegram handles, URLs, email IDs or social-media URLs, I4C also provides a dedicated suspect-reporting facility. Cyber Crime Portal
I4C Suspect Reporting Facility
For investment offers, I4C advises avoiding unsolicited WhatsApp/Telegram investment groups and checking whether an investment adviser is actually registered with SEBI. Cyber Crime Portal
The biggest new student-specific signal today is the college-student mule-account recruitment case. Scamfy should treat “easy commission for using/arranging your bank account” as a high-priority scam pattern, distinct from ordinary fake-job fraud.
